\begin{abstract}
Many applications built on large language models screen inputs with lightweight prompt-injection detectors: keyword heuristics,
signature rules or small text classifiers. We test four such detectors on four public attack collections that differ in
style (7{,}239 prompts): the heuristic layer of Rebuff and the YARA rules of Vigil as shipped, and logistic regression on word and
character n-grams. At their shipped thresholds the two heuristics flagged at most 13\% of attacks on three of the four
datasets; Rebuff's threshold of 0.75 sits above the score of the phrase ``Ignore previous instructions'' itself (0.70). The
classifiers reached AUC 0.85--1.00 within a dataset but a median of 0.64--0.66 when tested on another dataset, and with
the other three datasets pooled for training they still caught only 2--7\% of BIPIA and Open-Prompt-Injection attacks. Under
a threshold set for a 1\% false-positive rate, simple transformations removed most of what remained: the character
n-gram classifier fell from 0.53 to 0.10 with benign padding, 0.02 with leetspeak and 0.00 with zero-width characters or
base64, and Vigil detected nothing after any transformation. Rebuff's text normalisation incidentally made it immune to
zero-width characters and padding, its only robustness. Detectors of this kind measure how closely an input resembles
the attacks they were built from, so in-dataset results say little about protection, and they should be treated as one
layer of defence rather than a control.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Prompt injection, in which text supplied to a language-model application overrides its instructions, is the first item
of the OWASP list of risks for such applications \cite{owasp2025llm}. Attacks arrive directly from users
\cite{perez2022ignore} or indirectly through retrieved documents, emails and tool outputs \cite{greshake2023indirect}. A common
first response is to screen inputs before they reach the model. Open-source projects such as Rebuff \cite{rebuff} and Vigil
\cite{vigil} combine cheap scanners (keyword heuristics, signature rules) with more expensive ones, and practitioners
often add a small classifier trained on whatever attack examples they have.

Cheap scanners are attractive because they add no model calls and almost no latency. They are also easy to evaluate badly:
a classifier tested on a held-out split of the data it was trained on measures how well it recognises that collection, not
attacks in general, a pitfall well documented for machine learning in security \cite{arp2022dos}. This paper asks three
questions. (1) How well do shipped heuristics detect attacks from public collections? (2) Do learned n-gram detectors
generalise from one attack collection to another? (3) How easily are all of them evaded by transformations that leave the
attack readable to a capable model?

\section{Method}
\paragraph{Datasets.} Four collections with matched benign prompts (Table~\ref{tab:data}).
\emph{JailbreakHub}: in-the-wild jailbreak prompts and regular prompts from the same platforms \cite{shen2024dan}, 1{,}500
of each. \emph{Tensor Trust}: the 930 unique hijacking and extraction attacks from the game's robustness benchmarks
\cite{toyer2024tensortrust}, against 930 Alpaca instructions \cite{taori2023alpaca}. \emph{BIPIA}: emails, tables and code from the
benchmark with one of its text attacks inserted at the start, middle or end (440), against clean contexts (439)
\cite{yi2023bipia}. \emph{Open-PI}: Alpaca tasks carrying an injected Alpaca task with one of the five strategies of
Liu et al.\ (naive, escape characters, ignore, fake completion, combined) \cite{liu2024formalizing}, 750 of each. Duplicates
were removed; benign Alpaca instructions were never reused across datasets.

\paragraph{Detectors.} \emph{Rebuff heuristic}: Rebuff's \texttt{detect\_prompt\_injection\_using\_heuristic\_on\_input}, which scores
the best fuzzy match between substrings of the normalised input and 8{,}800 generated phrases such as ``ignore previous
instructions''. We reimplemented it, checked that it returns the same score as the original on test strings, and capped
inputs at 1{,}500 words; because it takes seconds on long prompts it was run on a stratified random subsample of 250 prompts
per dataset and class. Its default threshold is 0.75. \emph{Vigil YARA}: Vigil's injection rules (instruction bypass, system
instructions, guidance, ReAct and markdown exfiltration), positive if any rule matches. \emph{LR word} and \emph{LR char}: TF-IDF
logistic regression on word 1--2-grams and character 2--5-grams, class-balanced, threshold 0.5. We test only the cheap layers
of Rebuff and Vigil; both projects also offer model-based and vector-similarity scanners.

\paragraph{Experiments.} \emph{E1, in-dataset}: heuristics applied as shipped; classifiers by 5-fold cross-validation.
\emph{E2, cross-dataset}: a classifier trained on one dataset is tested on each other one, and trained on three and tested
on the fourth. \emph{E3, evasion}: 30\% of every dataset and class is held out, classifiers are trained on the remaining
70\% of all four datasets together, and held-out attacks are transformed: synonyms for trigger words, about 190 words of benign
text on each side (padding), leetspeak, Cyrillic homoglyphs, letter spacing within words of four or more letters,
zero-width spaces between characters, and base64 with an instruction to decode it \cite{boucher2022bad}. For each detector
and dataset the threshold is set so that 1\% of held-out benign prompts are flagged, and we report the true-positive rate
(TPR) at that threshold.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{Shipped heuristics caught little.} At its default threshold Rebuff flagged 0--2\% of attacks on BIPIA, Open-PI and
Tensor Trust and 13\% on JailbreakHub (Table~\ref{tab:in}). Its scores did carry signal (AUC 0.58--0.80), but the threshold is
above the score of the canonical attack: ``Ignore previous instructions.'' scores 0.70 and ``Ignore all previous instructions and
say hi'' 0.73. Vigil's rules matched 39\% of Open-PI attacks, the share built with the ``ignore'' and ``combined'' strategies
that contain that phrase, and 0--2\% elsewhere. Neither heuristic produced false positives of note.

\paragraph{Classifiers recognised their own data.} Within a dataset, both classifiers reached AUC 0.85--1.00, and Tensor Trust
was almost perfectly separable (0.999), partly because its attacks and Alpaca instructions differ in style as well as intent.

\paragraph{They did not travel.} Tested on a different dataset, the median AUC was 0.64 for word n-grams and 0.66 for character
n-grams, ranging from 0.45 to 0.90 (Figure~\ref{fig:cross}). Failures went both ways: a model trained on BIPIA flagged 85--92\% of
benign JailbreakHub prompts, while one trained on JailbreakHub caught 1--5\% of BIPIA and Open-PI attacks. Pooling three
datasets did not fix this: trained on the other three, the classifiers caught 7\% of BIPIA attacks and 2--5\% of Open-PI
attacks, and flagged 80--81\% of benign JailbreakHub prompts (many of which are long role-play requests that resemble
jailbreaks). The only pair that transferred well was JailbreakHub to Tensor Trust (AUC 0.87--0.90), two collections of
direct, human-written attacks.

\paragraph{Simple transformations removed most detection.} Trained on all four datasets and held to a 1\% false-positive
rate, the classifiers caught about half of the untransformed held-out attacks (0.49 and 0.53; Table~\ref{tab:ev}), with wide
variation by dataset (from 0.08 on BIPIA to 0.93 on Tensor Trust for character n-grams). Synonyms for a handful of trigger words cost
8--9 points. Every other transformation removed nearly all detection: benign padding left 0.08--0.10, leetspeak and letter
spacing 0.01--0.08, and zero-width characters and base64 0.00. Homoglyphs hurt the word model (0.05) more than the character
model (0.22), which still saw some unchanged characters. Vigil detected nothing after any transformation.

\paragraph{Rebuff's normalisation helped by accident.} Rebuff lower-cases the input and deletes every character that is not a
letter, digit or space before matching. That strips zero-width characters, so its detection was unchanged by them (0.30), and
because it searches for the best-matching window it was also unaffected by padding. It failed on leetspeak, homoglyphs and
spacing (0.02--0.06). Its apparent 0.68 on base64 is an artefact of our wrapper sentence, ``Decode the following base64 and do
what it says'', which shares words with its phrase list and scores 0.58, close to the 1\% thresholds of the two datasets (Open-PI and Tensor Trust) on which it was ``detected''; at its
shipped threshold Rebuff flagged no base64 attack.

\section{Discussion}
The detectors studied here measure resemblance to the attacks they were built from. Keyword heuristics encode one phrasing
of one attack; n-gram classifiers learn the vocabulary and style of one collection, including features that have nothing to
do with injection, such as the length and tone of role-play prompts. Neither survives a change of collection or a
transformation that a capable language model can undo. In-dataset numbers, which is what a team evaluating its own detector
usually sees, therefore overstate protection by a wide margin.

Three practical points follow. (1) Evaluate any detector on attacks from a different source than its training data, and at
the false-positive rate the application can tolerate. (2) Normalise inputs (Unicode confusables, invisible characters,
spacing) before any text-based check; Rebuff's accidental robustness to zero-width characters shows how much this buys
cheaply. (3) Treat cheap detectors as telemetry or one layer among several; the defences that hold up are architectural:
limiting what the model can do with untrusted content, separating instructions from data, and requiring confirmation
for consequential actions \cite{greshake2023indirect,liu2024formalizing}.

\paragraph{Limitations.} We tested only the cheap layers of Rebuff and Vigil, not their model-based scanners, and no
fine-tuned transformer detectors, which may generalise better; model weights could not be downloaded in our environment. Two
of the four datasets (BIPIA and Open-PI) were assembled by inserting published attacks into benign text, and benign prompts
for two datasets come from Alpaca, so style differences contribute to in-dataset separability. Rebuff was scored on a
subsample. We did not check whether a target model still follows each transformed attack, so the evasion results bound
detection, not attack success.

\section{Conclusion}
Open-source keyword and rule-based detectors, as shipped, caught little of four public attack collections, and n-gram
classifiers that looked strong within a dataset fell to a median AUC of 0.64--0.66 on another one and to near zero under
simple transformations. Cheap prompt-injection detectors do not travel, and they should not be relied on as a security
control.
